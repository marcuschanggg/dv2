Western Australia · January–December 2025

Twelve months of birds in Western Australia

Sightings logged by birdwatchers across Western Australia from January to December 2025 tell a story of who’s common, who’s rare, and how activity shifts with the seasons.

01 · Composition

What was seen

A breakdown of the taxonomic orders (the Latin group names, such as Psittaciformes for parrots) among the six species featured later in the story, from the largest to the smallest.

Reading clockwise from the top, Psittaciformes (parrots) make up almost half of the sightings, led by the Red-capped Parrot, followed by Passeriformes (songbirds) at about 30%, Coraciiformes (bee-eaters) at about 18% and Caprimulgiformes (frogmouths) at 3%. Together the first two orders account for roughly four in five sightings. These are the six species featured in section 05, logged between January and December 2025.

02 · Effort

Where the birds were counted

Sightings have been pooled into ~15km cells. More points mark wetlands and reserves where large flocks gather. Alongside the map, a look at which kinds of survey produced the records, and a calendar of when they came in.

About 85% of the points fall in the south-west corner, and more than half sit within 100 km of Perth. North of 23°S, across much of the state’s tropical north, there are only about 6%. The map shows where people watch birds as much as where birds live, so the empty areas are mostly unwatched rather than birdless.

Shore and backyard surveys account for about 98% of records, split almost evenly at 52% and 46%. Shore surveys also turn up the most species, 296 against 206 in backyards. Wetlands add only 1% of records, though they still yield 122 species.

Records climb through spring, with October and November alone holding a quarter of the year’s total, while August is the quietest month. Sundays are the busiest day, about 60% ahead of Tuesdays, which fits weekend birdwatching. Each square is one day of 2025 for the six species featured in section 05.

03 · Variety

Where the variety lives

Volume isn't the same as variety. Using real WA Regional Development Commission boundaries, darker blue means more distinct species in that region once the number of records is evened out, so that busy regions don’t win just by being watched more (Perth metro is excluded).

Allowing for how much each region was watched, the Kimberley is the most species-rich region, at about 146 species per 1,000 records. The Wheatbelt has the longest raw species list but falls to 92 because it is by far the most watched region, and the Pilbara is lowest at 73. This evens out the fact that busy regions log more species simply because more people are looking, so small gaps between the middle-ranked regions should not be over-read.

04 · Seasons

When the birds show up

Activity follows the calendar. See which season dominates each patch of ground, how each season divides among bird orders (spring is busiest, and songbirds lead in all four), which orders hold their place, and the daily rhythm of individual species.

In the south-west, spring is the busiest season in the largest share of areas, with summer leading around Albany and the south coast. North of about 23°S, almost every area peaks in winter, the dry season. The state effectively runs on two calendars: a spring surge in the south and a winter peak in the tropics.

Spring is the busiest season, holding about 30% of the year’s sightings against 22% in summer. Songbirds lead in every season, from 52% of summer sightings to 62% of spring’s. Parrots are a distant second in all four, between 10% and 12%.

Songbirds, parrots, waterfowl, pelicans and herons, and pigeons and doves hold the top five places in the same order all year. The movement is at the bottom, where kingfishers sit 10th in autumn and winter before climbing to 7th in spring. Hawks and eagles peak in winter, rising to 6th, then drop back to 10th in spring.

Four of the six species peak in October and the Tawny Frogmouth follows in November, so spring is the high point for almost all of them. The Rainbow Bee-eater is the most extreme, almost absent in April and May before surging in October. The Western Rosella is the exception, peaking in March.

05 · Species

Six species to watch

Some WA birds are homebodies; others range widely. The Western Rosella and Red-capped Parrot, both WA endemics, stay tightly confined to the South West, while the Rainbow Bee-eater ranges much further north.

The Western Rosella and Red-capped Parrot are confined to the south-west corner, with none of their records north of 30°S. The Regent Parrot is nearly as restricted, and the Splendid Fairywren stays almost entirely in the south. The Rainbow Bee-eater ranges furthest, with nearly a third of its records north of 30°S and some reaching the Kimberley.

06 · Change

A decade of change: 2015 vs. 2025

Comparing 2015 with 2025 for the 18 most-recorded species shows who’s thriving and who’s slipping.

Note: Western Gerygone sightings rose 143% over the decade; Magpie-lark fell 51%.

Eleven of the 18 species rose, led by the Western Gerygone, up 143% and climbing from 18th to 8th place. The Australian Raven moved to first place (+42%), displacing the Willie Wagtail, which fell to 5th after a 21% drop. The biggest loss was the Magpie-lark, down 51% and from 4th to 17th.
